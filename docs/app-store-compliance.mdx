This page explains what a published Restage surface can and can't change, and how that
relates to the store rules that usually come up. It isn't legal advice, and it doesn't
guarantee approval. You remain responsible for your app and how it uses remote content.

## What a published surface contains

A Restage surface uses the [Remote Flutter Widgets](https://pub.dev/packages/rfw) format.
What ships is data the app renders. Your widgets carry the logic, as Dart compiled into
your app. A published version composes and configures them. New behavior still ships as
an app release.

An update can change copy, layout, style values, flow routing, conditions and loops over
data your app supplies, and which registered event or action a control uses. Widget
implementations, gesture handlers, network clients, and native operations all come from
your app build.

## Apple App Review

[Apple's App Review Guideline 2.5.2](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/#software-requirements)
addresses downloading, installing, or executing code that introduces or changes
application features or functionality. A Restage surface carries no code. It uses widgets
and typed actions compiled into the app.

[Guideline 4.7](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/#mini-apps-mini-games-streaming-games-chatbots-plug-ins-and-game-emulators)
covers apps that offer certain forms of software not embedded in the binary, including
HTML5 mini apps and mini games. In Restage, native platform access lives in your own
widgets and registered host actions. Check whether your app's use of remote content falls
under any part of that guideline.

## Hidden and dormant features

[Guideline 2.5.19](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/#software-requirements)
asks that an app contain no hidden, dormant, or undocumented features, and that its
functionality be clear to users and to App Review.
[Guideline 2.3.1](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/#accurate-metadata)
asks that new features be described with specificity in the Notes for Review and be
accessible for review. Until October 16, 2026 both sentences lived in 2.3.1, so older
rejections cite that number. These are the guidelines most likely to come up for an app
that changes screens over the air, whichever tool delivers them.

Two properties of Restage help here. The surface you wrote is compiled into the build, so
a reviewer reaches it like any other screen. A published update arranges widgets and
actions already in that reviewed build, and new behavior ships as an app release.

The rest is yours to do:

- **Declare it.** Name Restage in the Notes for Review, list the screens it delivers,
  and say what a delivered update can change. A starting point:

  ```text
  This app uses Restage (restage.dev) to deliver layout and copy updates to these
  screens: <screens>. Each screen is compiled into this build and reachable at <path>
  with the demo account below. A delivered update is data rendered by widgets and
  actions already in this binary. Code, features, products and native capabilities ship
  in an app update. Which version a device receives depends on <the active version |
  a random split among the versions listed | rules on country, language or app build>.
  The review device is assigned like any other.
  ```

- **Keep every surface reachable.** Give the reviewer a route to each delivered screen:
  a demo account, no sign-up wall in front of it, no targeting rule that excludes the
  reviewer's device or region. The review device is a fresh install on a new device, so
  a rule on country, platform, device class or app build that such an install cannot
  match keeps the version from the reviewer too.
- **Keep review state out of host data.** Your app can pass values into a surface as host data. If
  the app computes a value to recognize a review device, from a sandbox receipt,
  TestFlight, an IP range or a remote "review mode" flag, and passes it in so the surface
  shows something different, that is review-environment detection, whatever delivers the
  flag. Third-party starter kits sometimes ship such a switch. Remove it before you
  submit.
- **Mind the names.** App Review reads the binary and what the app fetches during
  review, and a name is enough to raise a flag even when the code that carries it is
  unreachable on that platform. Flutter compiles every platform's branches into every
  build, so an Android-only switch is present in the iOS binary. Do not name a surface,
  version, audience, event, host data key or Dart symbol after review, reviewers or
  bypassing, and remember that a workaround built for one store's review ships to the
  other store's reviewers in the same build. Renaming or obfuscating such a switch makes
  it worse: 2.5.19 names hiding features in the binary "through code obfuscation or
  other methods" as grounds for removal and termination of the developer account.
- **Stay inside what was reviewed.** Copy, layout, ordering, styling and the choice among
  products and actions the reviewed build already offers are updates. A delivered surface
  that introduces a feature, a product or a claim the reviewed build did not have is a new
  feature under this guideline, and it belongs in a release with its own review notes.
- **Serve App Review what your users get.** Audience routing and experiments choose among
  published versions for your users. Using them to show a reviewer something different
  from what users receive can end a developer account. The guidelines' Introduction names
  trying to trick the review process as grounds for removal, and Guideline 2.3.1(b) makes
  repeated behavior grounds for removal from the Apple Developer Program.
- **Keep subscription disclosures in every version.** A published paywall keeps the
  disclosures [Guideline 3.1.2](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/#subscriptions)
  requires in every version: the price, the billing period, what renews, and how to
  cancel. A version that drops them breaks the rule even when reviewers and users see the
  same screen.
- **Treat reserved widgets as building blocks.** Carrying catalog widgets your app has not
  drawn yet, or a [reserve screen](/delivery/app-size), is ordinary: buttons, sliders,
  cards and layout are parts, and every design system ships parts it does not use on
  every screen. A widget that is itself a feature, such as a chat panel, a camera capture
  or a checkout, should be visible in the reviewed build or described in the notes before
  any delivered surface uses it.

### If App Review cites 2.5.19 or 2.3.1

App Review uses the phrase "review-environment detection" for code or configuration that
behaves differently when the app is under review. Rejections before October 16, 2026
cite 2.3.1(a) for it; newer ones cite 2.5.19, with 2.3.1(a) for the Notes for Review.
Check these in order: any condition on
sandbox receipts or TestFlight; routing rules a fresh install cannot match; host data
passed into a surface that depends on such a condition; third-party kits with a
review-mode switch; and your Notes for Review. In Resolution Center, name Restage, list
the delivered screens, and state how versions are assigned. The dashboard keeps the
version history and the routing rules for each surface; offer them as the record of what
was served to which audience.

## Google Play

[Google Play's device and network abuse policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16559646)
restricts applications that download executable code outside Google Play, such as dex,
JAR, and native library files. The Restage SDK reads surface data and renders it with
widgets compiled into the app.

For purchases during testing, add the accounts that will test your app as
[license testers](https://developer.android.com/google/play/billing/test) in Play
Console. Their purchases complete with test payment methods and are never charged, and
test subscriptions renew on an accelerated schedule. When your app gates content behind
a subscription or membership, Google asks for access details in the
[App access](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9859455)
section of Play Console: a test account and instructions, with up to five sets. Give the
reviewer an account that already holds the subscription, through a license tester
purchase or an entitlement you grant on the server. Do not add a way past the paywall
in the app itself. A bypass key or flag is what review systems look for, and it reaches
Apple's reviewers in the same build.

## Interaction stays in your app

Interactive catalog widgets can contain gesture, animation, or navigation logic. That
code ships with the app. The surface sets the widget's properties and receives its
events.

Host actions follow the same rule. Your app registers a named, versioned action with
typed arguments and results, and a flow can call it. The handler is Dart code compiled
into the app, so adding or changing one needs an app release.

## Compatibility and fallback

The compiler refuses source it can't express. On the device, the SDK refuses a published
version that doesn't match the widgets, custom widgets, or flow declarations in the
installed app. The app then shows the last version it accepted, the bundled copy, or its
fallback, and never a partial render.

These checks lower the chance of a broken update. They don't replace your own content
review or your reading of store policy. [Controlled delivery](/controlled-delivery)
describes rollback, freeze, and the audit log.
