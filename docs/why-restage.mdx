---
title: Why Restage
description: Author surfaces in the Flutter you already write, publish them without an app release, and keep your app in charge of behavior.
---

We built Restage so Flutter teams could update individual screens in a shipped app, measure each version, and see which one works best. Screens are built from your own widgets and design system. Add an annotation to a widget, and from then on you can publish new versions of it, test them against each other, and roll back the ones that don't work, all without an app release. It works the same way for onboarding, messages, surveys, paywalls and any other screen.

## Code first

Restage is code-first. You write surfaces in Dart, in your own repository, and they go through the same review, tests and version control as the rest of your app. There's no separate editor you have to keep in sync.

You use the Flutter widgets you already know. Theme reads, values like `TextStyle` and `BoxDecoration`, interactive state and your own components all carry through. `restage_codegen` follows the annotated widget tree, checks it against the widgets your app has installed, and writes a [Remote Flutter Widgets](https://pub.dev/packages/rfw) artifact. It also generates the mount that renders the rest of the screen from your app's code.

Your own components work too, with no annotation. If one is built only from catalog widgets, the build inlines it. If it has its own logic, it renders from your app. Mark one with `@RestageWidget` to name it, reuse it and list it in the editor. The build then splits a component with its own logic. Its layout and styling are delivered, and only the logic renders from your app. Its properties can be plain data classes and lists of structured values.

The compiler handles a bounded part of Flutter. When it can't represent something, it doesn't guess. It splits the screen: that part keeps rendering from your app and the rest is delivered. A few things still stop the build and point at the line. [Authoring constraints](/authoring/constraints) lists the limits.

## Your app stays in charge

What you publish is a description that your app's widgets draw. It's written in a small, closed language that the compiler checks. It can carry simple logic, like conditions over data your app supplies. Gestures, drawing, network calls and native work stay in Dart compiled into your app. New kinds of behavior still ship as an app release.

In practice, a surface can arrange catalog widgets, fire the events and actions your app registered, and set a flow's data and routing. Widget implementations, network access and action handlers come from your app build. The parts of a screen the build couldn't deliver also render from your app's compiled code. A surface fires your own callbacks by name.

We drew the line there on purpose. It gives the compiler clear rules to check, and it helps with app review. [App Store compliance](/app-store-compliance) covers that in more detail.

## A good fit for regulated teams

Every change you publish leaves a record you can check. That makes Restage particularly well suited to teams in health, finance and other regulated fields, where you need to show what was live and when:

- Each version is data you can read before it ships. It can't add behavior your app doesn't already have.
- Every delivered version is signed. The SDK renders it only after the signature checks out against a key built into your app. See [Signed delivery](/trust/signing).
- The [audit log](/controlled-delivery) records each change, who made it, which version it affected and why.
- A rollback restores an earlier version exactly. A freeze stops any version from going live until you lift it.

## It looks like the rest of your app

A surface that reads `Theme.of(context)` resolves it on the device, inside the running widget tree. It follows the app into dark mode, and it picks up a later design-system change without a rebuild. Where the design calls for a fixed color, you can use one.

## You see how each version does

<Callout type="info">
Analytics and A/B tests are in beta.
</Callout>

Once your app is connected to Restage Cloud, the SDK measures each published version. It records views, taps and completions on its own. You don't add any tracking calls.

Measurement is on by default. To switch it off, pass `measurementEnabled: false` to `Restage.configure`. [Telemetry and data](/trust/telemetry) lists what's recorded.

## When something fails, users still see a working screen

On the device, the SDK checks a fetched version against the installed app before it replaces what's on screen. If a check or a fetch fails:

- A generated screen mount shows your compiled widget.
- A paywall uses its last accepted version, a bundle, or your compiled widget.
- A flow uses its last accepted version, its bundled copy, or the `FlowUnavailablePolicy` your app chose.

[Live refresh](/delivery/live-refresh) of a surface that's already on screen goes through the same checks.

## One runtime for every surface

Onboarding, messages, surveys and paywalls all use the same compiler and runtime. You mount each one with its generated `*Surface(...)` widget. It comes with typed inputs and events, your compiled widget as the fallback, and an automatic placement. A flow with general delivery returns a `Map<String, Object?>`. Paywall purchase events go to your app, and your app runs the purchase with its existing store library or an adapter.

You can still mount `RestageScreen`, `RestagePaywall` and `RestageFlowGraph` directly. Then you supply the references and fallback setup yourself.

It doesn't have to be a whole screen. The same runtime works for a smaller region, like a card inside an existing `Scaffold`. Where you mount the generated widget decides which region can be delivered. Within it, the build delivers as much as it can, and `@RenderInApp` keeps more in your app.

## You own the output

You can bundle, commit and inspect the generated `.rfw` and flow files, and serve them from your own server or CDN. The SDK works without Restage Cloud.
